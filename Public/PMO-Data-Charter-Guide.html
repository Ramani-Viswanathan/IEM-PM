How to fill out the PMO Data Charter

The Charter is the one document where your judgment sets the rules, not the engine's. It tells IEM-PM what your data means before it compares anything against a standard. This page walks the template section by section — what each part is for, who actually writes it, and the mistakes that quietly undermine an audit before it starts.

First Charter: 45–90 minutes, mostly reviewing what Claude drafts for you.
Later audits, same organization: usually just re-ratifying with small edits.

Prerequisites

Before you start

The Charter interprets your data against your standards — so the standards have to exist first. Have these on hand before you open the template.

  • Standards already in knowledge/ The Charter's Materiality & Scope section is proposed from what the standards expect — not from your data. If knowledge/ is empty, there's nothing to propose it from yet.
  • A rough idea of what evidence you have You don't need it perfectly organized yet, but you should know roughly which schedules, logs, and reports exist for the project, program, or portfolio in scope.
  • The template itself skills/intelligence-engine/assets/PMO_DATA_CHARTER_template.md — copy it into your project's Audit/<ProjectName>/reports/ folder before editing, so the original template stays clean for next time.
  • Someone with authority to ratify Ratification is a real decision, not a formality — see §4.2. It should be made by whoever is accountable for how this audit's findings get used.

The Charter is not the baseline. The standards in knowledge/ are what your delivery data gets measured against. The Charter is the lens — it tells the engine what your data means so the comparison is fair. Get this backwards and every finding downstream inherits the error.

Machine-proposed, human-ratified

Who actually fills this in

In practice you rarely start from a blank template. Claude reads your standards and your data first, drafts a proposal for most sections, and your job is to review, correct, and ratify — not transcribe from scratch. Knowing which sections are proposed from what tells you where to focus your review.

Section Proposed from Your job
§1 Artifact Declaration Your data — the files actually supplied Confirm the list is complete; add waivers for anything genuinely out of scope
§2 Field Semantics Map Your data — how fields actually appear Correct any meaning the machine guessed wrong; you know your own RAG/status conventions, it doesn't
§3 Materiality & Scope The standards' expectations — never your data Set the thresholds that reflect your organization's actual risk appetite
§4 Propose → Ratify A summary of the above Sign it. This is the one step only a human can do.
§5, §6 Fixed — part of the contract itself Read and understand; nothing to fill in
§7 Appendix Both — standards list from knowledge/, assumptions from judgment calls made along the way Add any assumption or limitation the machine didn't think to flag

The one asymmetry that matters: Artifact Declaration and Field Semantics are safe to propose from your data, because you're reading the data to describe the data. Materiality & Scope is never proposed from your data — only from the standards. If scope came from what you happened to upload, a Missing gap could never surface, because the audit would only ever confirm what you already gave it.

Step by step

Section by section

Follow the template's own numbering — it's designed to be filled in this order.

Meta
2 minutes

Charter Metadata

The header table at the top. Most fields are self-explanatory, but three are worth getting right:

  • Audit Scope — be specific. "All projects" is rarely true; "Projects, PCS-EXAMPLE-001 only" or "Program X, all active projects" sets a boundary the rest of the Charter has to respect.
  • Charter Status — leave as PROVISIONAL until §4.2 is actually signed. Don't mark RATIFIED pre-emptively; that field is what the engine checks before it will run a non-caveated audit.
  • Charter Version — start at v1.0.0. Any later change to a ratified Charter needs a new version, not an edit in place (§6).
1
You review · machine proposes

1. Artifact Declaration

1.1 Artifact Inventory — every file that counts as evidence gets a row and a unique ART-XXX ID. Claude usually pre-fills this from what it finds in your evidence folder. Your job: check nothing is missing, and set Materiality honestly (Critical / High / Medium / Low) — this isn't decoration, it feeds directly into how seriously a gap in that artifact gets treated later.

1.2 Artifact Waivers — this is the section people skip, and it's the one that matters most. If a standard expects an artifact you don't have (a Resource Histogram, a standalone WBS, a Communication Plan), you have exactly two honest options: supply it, or waive it here with a real reason. Leave it undecided and it becomes a Missing gap by default — which is correct behavior, not a bug to work around.

Only what's explicitly waived here is out of scope. Silence is not a waiver.

2
You correct · machine proposes

2. Field Semantics Map

For every artifact in scope, define what its significant fields actually mean in your organization. This is the step that prevents the audit from guessing.

  • Does percent_complete mean physical % complete, duration % complete, or effort % complete? These are different numbers. Say which one.
  • What does your RAG status actually encode — schedule health, budget health, or an overall gut-check? If your organization uses a non-standard value (a "BLACK" status for crisis mode, say), record it in 2.5 Unmapped / Organization-Specific Fields rather than forcing it into Red/Amber/Green.
  • Leave Standard Mapping blank only if the field genuinely doesn't map to anything in your declared standards — an empty mapping is a legitimate answer, a guessed one isn't.
What "good" looks like — one row, filled correctly
| Field    | Type   | Meaning                          | Nullability | Business Rule           | Standard Mapping |
| -------- | ------ | --------------------------------- | ----------- | ------------------------ | ----------------- |
| `SPI`    | Calc.  | Schedule Performance Index = EV/PV | NOT NULL    | <1.0 = behind schedule | Standard for EVM  |

2.5 Unmapped Fields — anything the machine found in your data but couldn't confidently define goes here. Fields left here are not interpreted by the audit; they may be noted but will never drive a finding. That's deliberate — an unmapped field is a field the engine refuses to guess about.

3
You set, from the standards — not your data

3. Materiality and Scope

This is the section with the anti-mirror guard attached to it (see the callout above). Four sub-sections, all straightforward once you know the source of truth is the standard, not the upload:

  • 3.1 Organizational Scope — which business units, portfolios, programs, and projects are actually in this audit. "N/A — no program evidence supplied" is a legitimate, honest entry if that's genuinely the case.
  • 3.2 Time Horizon — your as-of date and lookback period. Keep this tight; a Week-6 status audit shouldn't silently pull in claims from Week 1 unless you say so.
  • 3.3 Materiality Thresholds — the numbers that decide what's "significant enough to be a finding." PMI's ±5% budget variance convention is a reasonable default; set it explicitly rather than leaving the bracket unfilled.
  • 3.4 Governance Levels — which boards/committees are in scope. If there's no Portfolio Board evidence, say No and why, not just leave it blank.
4
Machine proposes · only you can do 4.2

4. Propose → Ratify

4.1 is a summary of what got proposed and from where — read it, don't just skim past it. It's your last check before the number that matters: 4.2, the Ratification Statement.

This is not a rubber stamp. Signing it means: "I confirm this Charter accurately describes our delivery data, its meaning, and what matters." Every finding the audit produces afterward inherits whatever this statement got wrong. Read §1–§3 for real before you sign, especially the waivers and the materiality thresholds.

Short on time? You can decline to ratify and still run the audit — it just goes PROVISIONAL, and every finding carries a caveat that it rests on unratified interpretation. Fine for a first look. Not something to present at a steering committee.

5–6
Read only — nothing to fill in

5. Anti-Mirror Guard & 6. Charter Change Control

§5 is a fixed table restating the rule from §3's callout — it's there so anyone reading the Charter later can verify the guard was actually respected, not taken on faith. Nothing to edit.

§6 tells you what happens if something changes mid-audit: a ratified Charter is immutable for that run. New evidence that contradicts it doesn't get quietly absorbed — the audit halts, the Charter gets a new version, and it's re-ratified before anything continues. If you're the ratifier, this is the section that tells you when you'll be asked to sign again.

7
You add what the machine can't know

7. Appendix

  • 7.1 Declared Standards — usually auto-filled from knowledge/. Check the Applicability column makes sense — does your internal methodology actually override PMBOK where they conflict, or the other way around?
  • 7.2 Glossary — define any org-specific term used elsewhere in the Charter (an internal acronym, a non-standard status value) so a reader six months from now doesn't have to guess.
  • 7.3 Assumptions — the honest one. If you're assuming a 5-day work week, or that an unnamed org isn't the same client referenced elsewhere in your evidence, write it down here with what breaks if you're wrong. This section is what lets someone later understand why a finding turned out to be right or wrong.
  • 7.4 Known Limitations — what the evidence set can't tell you (e.g. "only a Week-6 snapshot, no access to Weeks 1–5"). This scopes what the audit can honestly claim, rather than letting a reader assume more coverage than actually exists.
Learned the hard way

Common mistakes

Every one of these has actually produced a bad audit outcome in a real run.

Leaving a waiver undecided

Silence in §1.2 isn't neutral — an unwaived, unsupplied artifact becomes a Missing gap. If you meant to exclude it, say so and why.

Fix: every expected-but-absent artifact needs an explicit disposition, not a blank row.

Setting scope from what you uploaded

Defining Materiality & Scope from your own evidence set is the anti-mirror guard's exact failure mode — the audit can only ever confirm what you gave it, and Missing gaps disappear structurally.

Fix: §3 comes from the standards' expectations, always. If you're not sure what the standard expects, that's what Stage 0's applicability map is for.

Ratifying without reading §1–§3

§4.2's statement is a real claim about accuracy, not a formality before the "real" work starts. A rushed signature just moves the error further downstream, where it's harder to trace.

Fix: if you don't have time to review, run PROVISIONAL instead — an honest caveat beats a false ratification.

Guessing at field meaning instead of leaving it unmapped

Forcing a field into a definition you're not sure about (e.g. assuming percent_complete is physical % when it's really effort %) doesn't remove the ambiguity — it just hides it as false confidence.

Fix: an honest entry in §2.5 Unmapped Fields is safer than a wrong one in §2.2–2.4.

Editing a ratified Charter in place

Once §4.2 is signed, the Charter is immutable for that audit run (§6) — quietly changing a threshold or a waiver after the fact breaks the audit trail between what was agreed and what was measured.

Fix: bump the version, re-ratify, and let §6's Emergency Changes process handle it if it happens mid-run.

Treating materiality thresholds as boilerplate

Copying the template's example percentages without thinking about your organization's actual risk appetite either buries real variances under a threshold that's too loose, or floods the Gap Register with noise under one that's too tight.

Fix: set §3.3 deliberately, and record the rationale — the column is right there for a reason.

Before you ask

Good to know

Do I have to write this from a blank template every time?
No. Ask Claude Code to propose a first draft from your standards and evidence — most people only ever review and correct §1–§3, then sign §4.2. Starting from a blank template is the exception, for when you want full manual control.
Can I reuse one Charter across multiple projects?
Yes, and it's the recommended pattern for a program or portfolio — see the User Guide's "By role" section for Program Managers and PMO leads. Run one audit per project, all against the same ratified Charter, so findings are comparable.
What happens if I skip §1.2 waivers entirely?
Every standards-expected artifact you didn't supply and didn't waive surfaces as a Missing gap. That's correct, intended behavior — not a bug, and not something worth "fixing" by leaving the waiver decision blank.
Where does the ratified Charter actually get saved?
Audit/<ProjectName>/reports/PMO_DATA_CHARTER_ratified.md — that specific filename and location is what the audit checks for before it will treat the Charter as ratified rather than provisional.
Can I change my mind after ratifying?
Yes, but not by editing the file in place. Bump the Charter Version (§4.2 gets a new sign-off), and if it happens mid-audit, the current run halts and restarts under the new version (§6) — findings from the old version are discarded, not merged.
Does the person who fills out §1–§3 have to be the same person who ratifies §4.2?
No — in fact it's often better if they're not. Someone close to the data can propose and correct the detail; ratification should come from whoever is accountable for how the findings get used, which is frequently a different role.
Never break these

The 5 rules

  1. Scope and materiality come from the standards, never your data. That's the whole anti-mirror guard, in one sentence.
  2. Silence is not a waiver. Every expected-but-missing artifact needs an explicit disposition in §1.2.
  3. An honest "unmapped" beats a confident guess. Fields you're not sure about belong in §2.5, not force-fit into a definition.
  4. Ratification is a real claim, not a formality. Read §1–§3 before signing §4.2 — every finding downstream inherits what it got wrong.
  5. Once ratified, immutable. Changes get a new version and a new ratification, never a silent edit.
